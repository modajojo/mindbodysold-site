Across the Strait: The Johor–Singapore Growth Story

A working hypothesis about economic development, the business services that growth may require and whether one of those businesses could become the foundation for an operator-led acquisition.

3 October 2026

Singapore and Johor are separated by a narrow strait, yet combining their economic strengths is much harder than simply crossing the Causeway.

Updated 29 October 2026; updated again 5 November 2026.

Working draft

This article sets out a hypothesis before primary research. I have published it to invite feedback and discussion. As the evidence develops, so too will the argument.

Across the strait

Stand on the waterfront in Johor Bahru after the evening rain has cleared and the wind comes clean off the water. The high-rises of Singapore emerge across the Johor strait, their lights hard and bright against the dark.

The distance between the two shores is small, less than a kilometre across the strait, yet the economic distance is rather greater.

Since the separation in 1965 the two countries have taken different roads. Singapore poured money into roads, schools, factories and the machinery of trade. Foreign capital came. It became a place of finance, of ships, of advanced manufacture. Malaysia built its own factories, especially in electronics, yet kept a heavier reliance on commodities and the land. Income and productivity followed different curves. Today Singapore’s nominal product per head stands more than seven times Malaysia’s, though purchasing power narrows the gap (World Bank, 2026).

It is a stark difference between peoples and businesses whose histories touch at every point. Yet the difference itself may hold an opening. Singapore has capital, technical skill and the long reach of international commerce, but land is scarce and costs run high. Johor has space, labour and an industrial base already in place, yet lacks the same density of capital and high-value enterprise.

The two shores hold capabilities that might fit together. Singapore has accumulated capital, technical expertise and international commercial relationships, while its geographical constraints and high operating costs limit the expansion of some activities. Johor offers land, labour and an established industrial base, but lacks the same concentration of capital and high-value corporate activity.

The question is whether drawing them closer can produce work that neither could manage as well alone. And, for the purpose at hand, whether that work might grow locally owned firms whose value lasts beyond the first wave of foreign money.

An experiment in economic integration

The Johor-Singapore Special Economic Zone (JS-SEZ), agreed in January 2025, is an attempt to join the complementary strengths of the two shores. Eleven sectors fall under its terms: manufacturing, logistics, digital services, business services, energy, education, healthcare and the rest. The governments aim to back fifty projects in five years, a hundred in ten, and to create twenty thousand skilled jobs (Singapore Ministry of Trade and Industry, 2025, Annex A).

The proposition is straightforward enough. Singapore brings capital, regional headquarters, international markets and specialised knowledge. Johor brings space, labour, manufacturing capacity and lower operating costs. Better transport, simpler administration and closer institutional cooperation should let firms place each part of their work where it can be done most effectively. Leadership, finance and commercial functions might stay in Singapore; production, operations or support might move across the water (Singapore Ministry of Trade and Industry, 2025).

There is nothing especially novel about this arrangement. For decades multinationals have divided their work between countries to exploit differences in cost, labour, skill and market access. I am currently driving a version of this process within the advertising industry, where work previously performed by local agencies has increasingly been transferred to regional or offshore delivery centres.

On paper the savings look large. In practice the move brings costs and frictions the original calculations often miss. Knowledge must travel. Teams must learn to work across distance and different organisations. Quality and the expectations of customers must be held while the old working relationships are broken and remade. The predicted gains can be real; so can the price of getting the transition wrong.

The JS-SEZ faces a related challenge on a much larger scale. The benefits of integration rest on more than proximity or the goodwill of governments. They rest on the capacity of businesses, institutions and people to coordinate their work across two distinct legal, political and economic systems. A railway may shorten the crossing. It cannot, by itself, remove the difficulties of doing business across the water.

The promise of investment

There are reasons to believe the ground is shifting in southern Malaysia. The country has long held a place in the world’s manufacturing chains, especially in electronics and electrical goods. Lately, money has begun to flow into digital infrastructure, adding another layer to its industrial life.

Data centres have drawn particular notice in Johor. These buildings house the machines that run cloud services, digital communications and, more and more, the training and operation of artificial intelligence. They demand heavy capital to build and, once running, a steady supply of power, cooling, telecommunications and specialised skill.

Bank Negara Malaysia reports that operational capacity rose from fifty-five megawatts in 2019 to seven hundred and eighty-four by the third quarter of 2025 (Bank Negara Malaysia, 2025, p. 21). Approved investment in the sector reached a hundred and forty-four billion ringgit between 2021 and the middle of 2025 (Bank Negara Malaysia, 2025, p. 21). Electrical and electronic exports climbed to seven hundred and eleven billion ringgit in 2025, up more than eighteen per cent from the year before (Ministry of Investment, Trade and Industry Malaysia, 2026, Table 6).

The numbers carry weight and yet they must be read with care. Approved investment is not the same as money spent and buildings finished. An expensive facility does not automatically create jobs or local activity in proportion to the capital it swallows. A data centre can take vast sums and, once operating, need only a handful of people. Much of its equipment arrives from abroad. Specialist maintenance and management often go to established international firms.

The scale of the capital, impressive as it is, tells us little about how much value will stay in the surrounding economy. The distinction matters. Malaysia’s industrial story is, in part, the movement from commodities toward more complex manufacturing and services. Greater participation in global production does not, by itself, mean greater ownership of the most valuable parts of that production. A country may build sophisticated goods without controlling their design, their intellectual property, their distribution or the commercial relationships that capture the largest profits.

Foreign capital can bring employment, skills and openings for local suppliers. It can also deepen reliance on foreign technology, foreign customers and foreign decisions about where money goes next. Both effects can run at the same time. The question, then, is not simply how much investment Johor draws. It is what Johor becomes able to produce, to supply and, in the end, to own.

Where might the value settle?

Consider a multinational company setting up a new operation in Johor. It needs land, buildings, machinery, power, workers and a way to reach its markets. Those needs create demand for construction, logistics, recruitment, maintenance and a string of supporting services. Some of the money will reach the local economy. But how much?

The company may bring its own suppliers from elsewhere. Purchasing decisions may sit with a regional headquarters in Singapore or with a global team thousands of miles away. Specialist work may go to firms that already serve the multinational in other markets. Local businesses may take only a thin slice of the available contracts, or find themselves competing where price counts for more than skill.

Or the local suppliers may grow capabilities that let them compete for more valuable work and, in time, reach beyond the companies or industries that first sustained them. The difference between these two paths is central to the hypothesis I’m exploring.

Economic growth can create demand. Demand does not, by itself, produce profitable openings for independent firms. A rising market does not guarantee that the companies serving it will become more valuable. One supplier may lift its revenue while remaining tied to a single customer. Another may lock in recurring contracts yet hold little power over price. A third may be highly profitable today and discover tomorrow that technology has made its services easy to replace.

Growth measures the size of an opportunity. It does not necessarily reveal who holds the power to capture it. Value creation and value capture are not always achieved by the same groups.

The attraction of business services

The first hypothesis was that rising industrial and commercial activity along the Johor-Singapore corridor would create demand for supporting business services: technical maintenance, engineering inspection, facilities management, recruitment, training, specialised operational support. These businesses share an apparently attractive trait. Their customers often need these services repeatedly. A production line must be kept running. A facility must stay open. Staff must be trained, equipment checked, regulations met.

But necessity alone does not guarantee profitability.

A customer may depend on a service and still hold the stronger hand. Large firms use competitive tendering, the weight of their purchasing and the existence of alternatives to drive prices down. A business can supply something essential and still earn thin returns.

There is also a practical consideration.

I have spent more than 20 years working in advertising, creative services, strategy and operational delivery, including global and regional leadership roles within WPP. This gives me a working knowledge of the economics and the daily difficulties of people-led service firms. More recently, my work has involved organisational change, the redesign of operating models, international delivery and the introduction of generative AI across a workforce of more than 2,000 people. I understand something of the economics and operational difficulties of people-led service businesses. I do not have the same experience operating industrial facilities or managing specialist engineering companies.

An acquisition in a foreign country and an unfamiliar industry would mean learning both at once while carrying responsibility for the firm’s results. Possible, perhaps, but not the most sensible place to start. A more credible beginning lies closer to existing experience.

One possibility is a Malaysian company providing managed marketing operations, content production or other technology-enabled business services to regional and multinational customers. These firms occupy a quieter corner of the marketing industry. They adapt campaigns for different markets, produce digital assets at scale, maintain content libraries, coordinate suppliers or run the processes that move marketing work from conception to delivery. Much of the labour happens out of sight. Its importance is often noticed only when something goes wrong.

The customer is not buying individual creative ideas. It is buying a reliable operating capability—people, systems, knowledge and relationships held together. That distinction may be commercially important. A company whose employees understand a client’s brands, processes and operational requirements may become difficult to replace, even when competitors offer lower prices, yet this assumption needs evidence.

Large multinational customers may already buy these services through global agency agreements that leave little room for independent Malaysian suppliers. Smaller firms may work as subcontractors, performing tasks with limited commercial security or bargaining power. And the sector itself may be shifting faster than its present business models can follow.

Which brings us to AI.

Technology and the problem of value

Generative AI offers service companies a way to raise productivity, to automate the repetitive and to cut costs. It also presents a threat to the basis on which many of these companies compete.

In coursework at Imperial College Business School the adoption of generative AI in the marketing services industry was examined through the lens of game theory, to see the incentives that face each firm (Jones, 2024). The difficulty is that what looks rational for one company can produce a different result when every company acts the same.

Picture a service provider that brings AI into its production. Time falls. Costs fall. Margins may improve. The firm can keep the savings, pass some to the customer or use the new advantage to take more work. Its rivals feel the pressure and follow. Once the tools are widespread the early edge fades. Customers notice the productivity gains and press for lower prices. Competition shifts some, perhaps most, of the economic benefit from the suppliers to the buyers. The industry grows more productive without growing more profitable.

That creates a problem for anyone thinking of buying a service business. Raising the firm’s productivity may be entirely possible. Keeping the financial reward of that rise is another matter. If the company’s value rests mainly on doing routine work more cheaply than others, AI can undercut it. If the value rests on accumulated knowledge, specialised judgement, trusted relationships and the capacity to coordinate complex work, technology may reinforce it.

Even those strengths cannot be taken as permanent. Some knowledge can be written down. Some judgement can be automated. Customer relationships may matter less when other suppliers can show comparable quality at a markedly lower price. The businesses most likely to last are those that understand what the customer is really buying and can keep supplying something hard to replace. That may not be the same service they sell today.

It is also a reason to look past revenue growth and operating margins when a company is weighed. The source of its economic advantage must be understood, how that advantage is held, and what might cause it to vanish.

The difficulty of acquiring what matters

There is a further difficulty, quieter and more stubborn than the others.

Even if one finds a company that works — sound in its numbers, attractive in its returns — the act of buying it may wound the very things that gave it strength. This was one of the central concerns of my research into mergers and acquisitions in the advertising industry (Jones, 2025).

A buyer can examine financial statements, contracts, customers, employees and intellectual property, and conclude that it has a reasonable understanding of the business being purchased. Yet some of its most important assets may be difficult to identify, let alone value. The founder who has spent twenty years earning the quiet confidence of his clients. The experienced employee who understands how to resolve a difficult problem. The unspoken understandings between colleagues that let the work move forward without constant intervention.

These are productive capabilities. They may not appear in the model used to justify the purchase, yet they shape the company’s ability to earn future profits. An incoming owner often arrives with plans of more efficient systems, tighter control, standardised working practices. Some of these changes may be necessary. Others may, without meaning to, take apart the fragile arrangement that allowed the business to succeed in the first place. The line between the two is not always obvious.

Studies of professional-service acquisitions have looked hard at how integration is handled, how the people behave under new ownership, and what happens to performance afterward (Pickering, 2017; Bauer et al., 2024).

For an operator-led acquisition, this raises a rather uncomfortable question. How can a new owner claim to know how to improve a business before fully understanding what makes it work?

The urge to bring in the systems and habits learned in a large multinational can be strong. Practices designed to hold thousands of people in line may sit poorly on a firm of thirty or forty, whose success rests on different loyalties and different motives. A measure of humility seems required. The power to write a cheque does not bring with it understanding. The ability to buy a company does not confer an understanding of it. Nor does legal ownership guarantee the continued cooperation of the employees, customers and suppliers whose knowledge and relationships sustain its value.

An acquisition might offer a chance to make a business better. It might equally become a costly education in why the previous owner did things the way he did.

Ownership, work and economic power

Beneath all of this lies a wider question. In a people-led service business most of the productive value is made by the knowledge, labour and relationships of those who work there. Ownership, however, gives shareholders control of the company and a claim on whatever profit remains after the obligations are met.

The distinction between earning income through work and earning returns through ownership has occupied much of my recent thinking about capitalism and economic history. Labour generally provides income in exchange for time, effort and skill. Capital can generate returns that may subsequently be reinvested to acquire more productive assets. Over time the two mechanisms can open substantial differences in economic power.

An employee’s freedom to negotiate better terms depends in part on the alternatives open to him. An employer’s power to set those terms depends on its own standing in the market. Neither operates independently of the other. The relationship becomes especially interesting when ownership changes.

A new investor may bring in technology, reorganise the work or shift activities to lower-cost places, raising productivity and, potentially, the returns available to shareholders. Employees may gain better systems, stronger career paths or a more secure firm. They may equally find that the skills they have built become less valuable, or that the economic gains from higher productivity flow mainly to owners and customers.

None of these outcomes is fixed. They turn on the choices management makes, the competitive pressures the business faces and the relative bargaining power of those involved. This article is not the place for a broader argument about the merits of capitalism or the distribution of wealth; those questions need their own examination. But they are relevant to how I think about ownership.

If the value of a business rests on its people, then the way an incoming owner treats those people is not merely an ethical matter. It is a commercial one. Employees can leave. Customers can follow them. A contract can transfer legal ownership of a firm, but it cannot compel the continued trust of the people who make the firm work.

The search-fund proposition

A search fund is one possible route to acquiring and running an established private company. In its usual form an individual or a small team raises money to finance the search for a suitable business, secures further capital to complete the purchase, and then takes responsibility for managing the firm (Kelly et al., 2026).

The model holds an obvious appeal for an experienced operator who lacks the personal capital needed for a substantial acquisition, a position which your author finds himself in. Instead of starting from nothing, the operator buys a company that already has customers, employees, revenue and a record of commercial life. Yet the existence of an established business does not remove uncertainty.

A firm may have thrived under its founder for decades and still falter when ownership changes. Its customers may be concentrated. Its working-capital needs may be poorly understood. Its results may depend on favourable conditions that will not last. The price paid matters as much as the underlying quality of the company. An excellent firm bought at an excessive valuation can still prove a poor investment.

Malaysia adds practical questions of its own: foreign ownership rules, licensing, financing and the willingness of founders to sell to an incoming operator. Then comes the matter of credibility. Why should a Malaysian owner, after years spent building a company, hand its future to someone who has never before run a business in the country? A prospective buyer needs an answer that goes beyond access to capital or the promise of better management. That answer must rest on a credible understanding of what the business needs and what the new owner can contribute.

For me, the attraction of an operator-led acquisition lies in the chance to join the established capabilities of a sound firm with experience in organisational change, operational improvement and the adoption of technology. Whether that combination would in fact create value is something still to be shown.

Why Johor?

There remains a final question that could kibosh the whole argument. Why should the business be in Johor?

If managed marketing operations or another people-led service sector offers an attractive chance to acquire a company, equally suitable firms may exist in Kuala Lumpur, Penang or elsewhere in Southeast Asia. The existence of an economic zone does not, by itself, make Johor the best place to buy such a firm. The corridor would need to supply a real advantage.

Perhaps proximity to Singapore gives Malaysian suppliers better access to regional headquarters and multinational customers. Perhaps the combination of available skills, competitive operating costs and improving transport links creates an attractive environment for delivering services across Southeast Asia. Or perhaps those same advantages may already exist elsewhere, independent of the success of the special economic zone.

I have not established which explanation is correct. The distinction matters. There is a risk of mistaking an interesting economic development for a compelling investment opportunity. The two may be connected. They may also be largely separate. A sound acquisition thesis should be able to survive that difference.

From hypothesis to evidence

The argument has come as far as secondary sources and this author’s own experience will carry it. Beyond that point the ground grows uncertain, and one must walk carefully.

There is money moving into Malaysia, real money, substantial enough to alter the shape of the land between Johor and Singapore. The corridor tightens. Factories rise. Roads thicken. The two places lean toward each other like neighbours who have decided, after long years of polite distance, to share a fence. Whether that closeness will throw off steady work that independent Malaysian firms can take and keep at a profit remains another matter. Whether any of those firms would bear the weight of an outsider’s ownership is still less certain.

I intend to investigate this through a programme of research built around several questions.

First: what does the corridor change?

Does the special economic zone open doors that were not already ajar in Kuala Lumpur or Penang, or does it merely rearrange the furniture of an old room? I need to see the place as it is, not as the maps and press releases would have it.

Second: what are the buyers truly purchasing, and for what reasons that do not appear in the ledgers?

Investment figures reveal little about the private calculations of a procurement manager who stays with one supplier year after year. I will talk to those who place the orders, who manage the contracts, the suppliers who live by them. I want to know what work is sent outside, what troubles them and why loyalty settles where it does.

Third: what makes such a business worth keeping?

This means examining customer relationships, recurring revenue, margins, management capability, technological exposure and the factors that allow a company to retain a share of the value it creates.

Fourth: could I improve one?

I need to distinguish between the experience that might transfer from my existing career and the knowledge I would have to acquire. This may ultimately prove as important as the choice of sector.

Finally, could an acquisition be made on sensible terms?

The research must eventually confront the economics of ownership: valuations, financing, cash flow, succession, regulatory requirements and the willingness of founders to sell.

I shall treat these questions as linked hypotheses, each one to be pressed against rival explanations until something gives. The research will begin with a comparative examination of the market, followed by conversations with buyers, founders and investors. Those findings will determine which sectors, if any, merit deeper investigation.

There is no particular reason why the answer must be marketing operations. There is no necessity that the best chance, if one exists, must sit in Johor. The evidence may lead elsewhere. It may also lead to the quiet conclusion that no purchase is warranted at all.

Where this leaves us

The corridor between Johor and Singapore is an experiment still half-formed. Two countries, divided by a narrow strip of water that can be crossed in minutes, are trying to join strengths that grew separately for generations, one along one path, the other along another. The money pouring in is large enough to suggest that something real may be taking shape. Yet capital, however abundant, does not always root itself in the soil it waters. Activity can rise and still leave behind no independent businesses strong enough to stand on their own, with advantages that endure after the first excitement fades.

For an investor, there is a considerable distance between identifying a growing market and buying a company capable of producing attractive returns. My working thought is that the corridor’s growth may open ground for service firms that lean on both technology and the quiet competence of people, that is, firms that help regional and multinational companies do their work. Somewhere in that space there may already exist a business whose relationships, skills and place in the market could serve as the base for an operator who means to stay and improve it.

I do not yet know if such a firm is there to be found, or if it could be bought on terms that make sense, or if I would be the right person to take it on. That is the purpose of the research.

The view across the strait is striking, particularly after the rain has cleared and the lights of Singapore begin to emerge, and from Johor Bahru, the economic promise on the opposite shore seems almost close enough to touch. Whether any part of that promise can be brought across the water and made to last on this side remains an open question, unanswered still.
