Singapore and Johor are separated by a narrow strait, yet combining their economic strengths is much harder than simply crossing the Causeway

A working hypothesis about the Johor-Singapore corridor, the business services its growth may require and whether one of those businesses could support an operator-led acquisition.

3 October 2026

Working draft

This article sets out a hypothesis before primary research. I’ve published it to invite feedback and discussion. It will change as evidence comes in.

Stand on the waterfront in Johor Bahru, Malaysia and after the evening rains clear you can see the distant high-rises and bright light of Singapore’s urban centre across the strait.

The short distance is crossed by road today. A new rapid-transit line is intended to cut the rail journey between Johor Bahru and Singapore to about five minutes, but passenger service is now expected to begin in February 2027, subject to safety certification and final regulatory approval (Land Transport Authority, 2026). The economic gap between the two countries, however, has continued to widen since Singapore decoupled itself from Malaysia economically during the late 1970s and 1980s. Malaysia’s industrial engine, powered by volatile commodity exports (oil, rubber, palm oil) faced structural bottlenecks that kept it in a “middle-income trap” (Cherif and Hasanov, 2019). In contrast, from 1960 to 1990, Singapore consistently allocated a significantly higher portion of its GDP to Gross Fixed Capital Formation (long-term public and industrial infrastructure) than Malaysia. By the early 1980s, these compounding growth dynamics transformed the gap from a modest step into an exponential leap. Today, the economic realities of the two nations exist on entirely different planes. While Malaysia’s total national economy remains competitive in overall nominal size due to its large population, Singapore’s GDP per capita has widened to over 7 times that of Malaysia’s, positioning it as one of the richest nations globally (World Bank, 2026).

The structural results of this economic separation are starkly highlighted by contemporary macroeconomic indicators

Constraints and imperatives.

Singapore is a global centre for finance, trade and regional corporate headquarters. It has capital, customers and skilled management but little land and high operating costs. Johor is Malaysia’s southernmost state. It offers more space, a large workforce and lower costs, with road links into Singapore through the Causeway and the Second Link.

The Johor-Singapore Special Economic Zone, usually shortened to JS-SEZ, is an attempt to make those differences useful. The premise is simple: allow each side to contribute what it has, reduce some of the friction between them and create a larger economic area.

For an investor, the first conclusion is tempting. More factories, data centres and regional operations should mean more work for the companies that support them. Find a fragmented service sector, buy a sound local business and build a larger group around it.

That is the hunch.

Where we are

Johor sits at the southern tip of Peninsular Malaysia. Singapore lies immediately below it. People, goods and money already move between the two every day, although border congestion and separate legal systems create real friction.

Malaysia and Singapore signed the formal JS-SEZ agreement in January 2025. The two governments aim to support 50 projects in its first five years and 100 within ten years, with the stated intention of creating 20,000 skilled jobs. The zone covers 11 sectors, including manufacturing, logistics, digital services, business services, energy, education and healthcare. It is not a single industrial park. It is a large designated area containing several development zones, each with its own emphasis and incentives (Singapore Ministry of Trade and Industry, 2025; Malaysian Investment Development Authority, 2025).

The idea is bigger than cheap land next to an expensive city. Singapore wants room for companies to expand while keeping high-value decisions and capabilities anchored at home. Malaysia wants investment, skilled work and stronger local industries. A company might keep its regional leadership in Singapore while placing production, operations or support teams in Johor.

That sounds neat on paper. Running a business across a national border rarely is.

What is changing

Malaysia already has a substantial manufacturing and services economy. It is deeply connected to global supply chains in electronics, machinery, chemicals and other traded goods. The World Bank describes a country that has moved far beyond its earlier dependence on agriculture and commodities and is approaching high-income status (World Bank, 2025).

Johor is now receiving a visible share of the next wave of investment. Data centres are the clearest example. These are the large, secure buildings that hold the computer servers behind cloud computing, streaming, online services and AI. They need electricity, water, cooling systems, network connections, security and constant maintenance.

Bank Negara Malaysia (2025) reports that Malaysia’s live data-centre capacity rose from 55 megawatts in 2019 to 784 megawatts by the third quarter of 2025. Approved data-centre investment reached RM144.4 billion between 2021 and the first half of 2025.

Electrical and electronic products, often abbreviated to E&E, provide another part of the picture. The category includes semiconductors, components, industrial electronics and consumer electrical goods. Malaysia’s Ministry of Investment, Trade and Industry (2026) reports that E&E exports reached RM711.61 billion in 2025, up 18.3 per cent on the previous year.

These figures show momentum. They do not show what will endure.

Some are national figures, not Johor figures. Approved investment is a commitment, not a completed factory or operating data centre. Large digital facilities absorb great sums of capital but employ fewer people than conventional factories. Projects can be delayed, reduced or cancelled.

The distinction matters. A press release can announce billions of ringgit in an afternoon. Building a functioning local economy around that money takes years.

The wider context

PESTLE is an ungainly acronym for a useful question: what is happening around the business that could help it, constrain it or change the result? It covers political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental forces. In this case, culture belongs inside the social and commercial setting, not in a separate box labelled “local colour”.

Political. The zone depends on cooperation between two countries and, within Malaysia, between federal, Johor state and local authorities. An investment facilitation centre has been created to coordinate approvals across those layers. That should reduce friction, but it also shows how many institutions may touch a project. Political support is real. Execution still matters.

Economic. The attraction comes from difference. Singapore supplies capital, regional demand and corporate headquarters. Johor offers land, labour and a lower operating-cost base. The model works only if the savings survive border delays, duplicated management, currency movements and the cost of coordinating work across two systems.

Social and cultural. Malaysia is multiethnic and multilingual. Bahasa Malaysia is the official language, while the government describes English as the dominant language of trade and industry; Mandarin, Tamil and other languages are also widely used (Government of Malaysia, 2026). There is no single Malaysian business culture that explains every company. Sector, ownership, generation and region all matter.

For an outside buyer, this is practical rather than ceremonial. A contract may transfer ownership. It does not automatically transfer an owner’s standing with customers, staff or suppliers. In a people-led service company, those relationships may be part of the asset being bought. Treating them as an obstacle to be cleared away would destroy value quickly.

Technological. Data centres, semiconductors, cloud services and AI are increasing demand for technical infrastructure and digital work. They are also changing how service companies operate. Automation may improve speed and margins, but it can also make routine work cheaper and easier for a customer to move elsewhere. Technology creates the opportunity and attacks it at the same time.

Legal. Malaysia permits full foreign ownership in many manufacturing and service activities, but the rules vary by sector. Licensing, professional restrictions, foreign-equity limits and Bumiputera participation requirements can affect the structure of a deal. The legal route for a marketing-operations company may be quite different from the route for engineering, logistics or a regulated professional service. “Malaysia” is not one set of acquisition rules.

Environmental. The fastest-growing sectors place pressure on physical systems. Data centres need large amounts of power and water, which is why Malaysia has introduced sustainability guidelines covering their development and operation (Malaysian Investment Development Authority, 2024). Energy supply, water use and public consent are business constraints, not side issues. A boom that strains the grid or local resources may face higher costs and tighter controls.

This wider setting explains why the corridor is interesting. It also explains why the opportunity cannot be read straight from the investment totals.

The opportunity

The hypothesis rests on six linked claims:

  1. Industrial and commercial activity in the corridor keeps growing.

  2. That growth creates recurring demand for outside business services.

  3. Some of those services need local delivery and are regularly outsourced.

  4. The suppliers include sound, owner-managed companies that can be bought.

  5. Common ownership can improve those companies without damaging the relationships and working habits that make them valuable.

  6. A particular operator has a credible reason to find and run such a company.

The first claim has reasonable public support. Each step after it is less certain.

A new factory does not guarantee work for a local supplier. A multinational may bring its existing vendors, buy through a regional contract managed in Singapore or keep important work inside the company. A growing market can still be closed to a new independent provider.

A market may also be fragmented for bad reasons. Small firms may depend on one founder, one licence or a handful of personal relationships. They may look like building blocks from a distance but prove impossible to combine. Put several weak firms together and the result is a larger payroll, not a stronger business.

“Growth plus fragmentation” sounds like an investment thesis. Usually, it is the beginning of the research.

The hard questions sit in between: what do customers buy repeatedly, who supplies it, why do customers stay and what survives when ownership changes?

From industrial growth to business services

The original version of the hypothesis pointed towards industrial support services. That could include equipment maintenance, engineering inspection, facilities management, technical recruitment or workforce training.

These sectors have an attractive feature. The work is often necessary rather than optional. A production line must be maintained. A regulated inspection must be completed. A facility must keep running.

They also expose a weakness in my case. I am not an industrial engineer, a data-centre operator or an expert in Malaysian regulation. Searching for a technical industrial company would combine a new country, a new sector and a first acquisition. Any serious investor should question the sense of taking all three risks together.

My own experience lies in people-led, multi-client service businesses. I have spent more than 20 years in creative, strategic and operational roles, including global and regional leadership within WPP. More recently, I have coordinated delivery across markets and agencies, redesigned operating models, moved work into offshore hubs and supported the adoption of generative AI by more than 2,000 employees.

That points to a narrower starting place: recurring, technology-enabled business services that help regional and multinational companies manage complex delivery.

A provisional target

The first working example is a Malaysian managed marketing operations or content-delivery company serving large businesses.

The phrase needs translating.

A conventional creative agency develops campaigns and ideas. Much of its income may come from individual projects or competitive pitches. A managed marketing-operations company sits closer to the client’s weekly machinery. It may adapt a global campaign for several countries, produce thousands of digital assets, manage approval workflows, maintain content libraries, coordinate suppliers or run parts of a company’s digital marketing operation.

The work is less visible than an advertising campaign. That may be the point. It happens every week, and failure causes delay, waste and reputational risk.

A suitable company might have:

  • repeat or contracted revenue from large clients;

  • recurring volumes and a real cost when a client changes supplier;

  • managers and delivery teams who can operate without the founder in every meeting;

  • regional or multinational customers;

  • limited dependence on media spending;

  • workflows where automation can improve speed, quality or margins;

  • the ability to serve Singapore-led clients from Malaysia.

This is adjacent to business-process outsourcing, usually called BPO. BPO companies take responsibility for a defined process that another company chooses not to run itself. The difference here is that the process would sit within marketing and content operations, where judgement, brand knowledge and client relationships still matter.

AI workflow implementation, workforce training or other managed services might fit beside the main offer later. That is a possibility, not a forecast. Each added service would need to solve a problem that customers already recognise.

There are serious objections. Multinationals may control this work through global procurement agreements. WPP network agencies are typical examples of where this arrangements take place**.** Malaysian firms may receive only overflow work with little margin or security. AI may force prices down faster than it reduces costs (I have a paper where we work through the game theory approach of how to think about this). Repeat work may disappear at the next procurement review. High client concentration and dependence on founders may be normal in the sector.

These objections belong in the centre of the argument. Hiding them at the end would make the thesis look cleaner and the thinking worse.

The search-fund question

A search fund is a way for an individual operator to acquire and run an established private company. The searcher raises money to find a business, brings in further capital to buy it and then becomes responsible for its performance (Kelly et al., 2026).

This is different from buying shares in a listed company. It is also different from a large private-equity fund hiring an existing management team. The operator’s own judgement, credibility and ability to lead the company are central to the case.

Good regional statistics will not carry that argument. Investors need to believe that the searcher can find a suitable company, persuade its owner to sell and improve the business afterwards. The owner needs to believe that the buyer will protect the company, its staff and its reputation.

That makes local knowledge especially important.

A good Malaysian company would already have clients, staff, operating knowledge and a practical understanding of how work gets done. Buying it would provide a base that an incoming operator could not reproduce quickly.

In return, the operator must offer something beyond money. The case might include clearer governance, better delivery systems, more effective use of distributed teams and careful automation of repetitive work.

Care is not a soft concern. My research into advertising mergers found that buyers often damage the informal capabilities they meant to acquire. Client trust, individual knowledge and the habits that hold a team together rarely appear in a deal model. Standardise too quickly and the source of the earnings can disappear. Peer-reviewed research on professional-service acquisitions likewise finds that client relationships and tacit knowledge are major sources of value, and that integration choices can affect professional behaviour and company performance (Pickering, 2017; Bauer et al., 2024).

The question is plain: can new ownership improve something the customer values without breaking the system that produces it?

How the idea fails

I would abandon or change the thesis if research showed that:

  • regional buyers have no important unmet need in these services;

  • procurement structures leave little room for independent Malaysian firms;

  • the available work is low-margin and easy to move;

  • attractive companies are too small or too dependent on their founders;

  • customer concentration makes an acquisition impossible to finance;

  • owners have little interest in succession, investment or sale;

  • suitable companies cost too much for a search-funded acquisition;

  • the legal or ownership route makes the target sector impractical;

  • my experience carries little weight with owners, customers or investors.

One failed link may be enough.

The work may point to a different sector. Technical training, specialist BPO, AI-enabled workforce services or another nearby market may prove stronger than marketing operations. The purpose is to find where customer demand, company quality and my experience meet. Protecting the first idea would defeat the point.

Before the fieldwork

The Johor-Singapore corridor appears to be changing in ways that will create work and new businesses. I do not yet know whether it will produce an acquisition opportunity suited to my background.

The hunch is this: growth in the corridor may support technology-enabled, people-led service companies that perform recurring work for large businesses from a Malaysian operating base. Somewhere in that market, there may be an owner-managed company that could become a stronger regional business without losing the relationships and working habits on which its value depends.

The next step is to speak with buyers, founders and investors. I want to know what customers buy repeatedly, what causes them trouble, what is hard to replace and where owners believe their companies are constrained.

The evidence may narrow the idea, send it elsewhere or end it.
